# Postcard Memory Poster — Art Direction

This is the English counterpart of `references/art-direction.md`. It describes the same established visual language for English-language documentation and readers.

## Core relationship

- TOP: the real photograph, clear and spontaneous.
- BOTTOM: the same moment remembered as a lighter, quieter watercolor.
- Both halves share the same place, action, emotional center, and dominant colors.

The watercolor is an extraction, not a complete redraw. It should remind the viewer of the exact moment without becoming a dense painted copy.

## Layout, paper, and color

- Use a vertical 3:4 canvas with a clear horizontal split of approximately 50/50.
- Keep the two halves visually unified, without a heavy frame or hard shadow.
- Use warm-white, pale-ivory, or lightly gray natural watercolor paper in the lower half.
- Keep visible paper grain, transparent pigment, wet-on-wet blooms, dry-brush marks, granulation, broken edges, and natural pooling.
- Extract roughly 3–5 dominant colors from the photograph and reduce saturation in the watercolor.
- Prefer charcoal over solid black, muted warm red over saturated red, pale sage over dense green, and soft indigo or gray-blue over heavy blue.
- Do not use a large digital gradient or a uniform watercolor filter as a substitute for paper and pigment.

## Top half: faithful photograph

Preserve identity, facial features, expression, hairstyle, body proportions, clothing, accessories, pose, gesture, movement, spatial relationships, camera perspective, lighting, and the original narrative. Apply only subtle editorial grading. If the canvas needs expansion, extend only environmental areas such as sky, ground, walls, vegetation, roads, or architecture.

When several people define the moment, preserve their relative scale, distance, direction, and interaction. Do not randomly merge, replace, or reposition them.

## Bottom half: watercolor memory

- Do not paint the entire background or create a rectangular painted backdrop.
- Retain the emotional center, essential silhouette, action direction, important objects, and a few environmental clues.
- Let unnecessary detail dissolve into paper, pigment, motion, and empty space.
- Scene-series artwork is usually medium-small, about 35%–60% of the lower-half width, with generous margins.
- Negative space and atmosphere take priority in the people series. Starting from a normal readable depiction, reduce the watercolor person to about 60%–75% of that visual scale; if the lower half still feels crowded, reduce below 60% until the paper becomes the dominant area.
- Do not turn the person into an unreadable dot: the facial mood, center of gravity, key gesture, and relationships must remain recognizable. When a larger figure conflicts with a better atmosphere, choose the smaller figure and more paper.
- Keep the environment lighter than the subject and avoid filling the whole lower half.

## Scene series — Postcard Scene

For cities, rivers, coasts, roads, architecture, nature, and sunsets, retain only 1–3 place-recognition clues:

- city skyline, landmark silhouette, bridge, riverbank, or street direction;
- water direction, reflections, shoreline, bridge, and a few boats;
- horizon, path, trees, fence, flowers, or a band of sunset color.

Keep the original perspective and major direction, but allow details to dissolve. The scene should feel like a loose watercolor cluster floating on paper, not a fully illustrated background.

## People series — Watercolor Motion

- The goal is not a finely rendered watercolor portrait; it is to capture a person in motion.
- Strictly preserve the photograph’s action skeleton, center of gravity, limb directions, pose, and trajectory.
- Use wet-on-wet, layered wet and dry marks, translucent color layers, and natural water traces.
- Deliberately keep motion blur: hair, skirts, garment edges, arms, legs, and sports equipment may trail along the physical direction of motion through pigment drag, softened edges, and repeated marks.
- Stronger motion may dissolve, fracture, or blur more at the edges; the body center, face mood, key joints, and essential object remain relatively clear.
- Use “partly clear + partly disappearing” contours. Do not trace every outline completely.
- A few fast, directional dry-brush sweeps may suggest speed, rotation, swing, or inertia, but never use comic-book speed lines.
- Motion blur must follow real physical direction, never random smearing.
- Fade the environment more than the people so the viewer looks at the action itself.
- Keep continuous ivory paper around the figure, especially above the head, along the gesture direction, and on both sides of the body. The person is a memory anchor; negative space and atmosphere are the main subject.

Build the watercolor from an action chain such as:

`equipment or starting point → arms and shoulders → torso rotation and weight → hair or clothing inertia → legs and landing point`

For golf, for example, the club direction, arm extension, torso rotation, skirt movement, and leg-weight shift should form one continuous watercolor motion chain.

The intended result is:

> Not “a person painted in watercolor,” but “a person in motion caught by watercolor.”

## Typography and information layer

- People series defaults to one short title line and one thin divider.
- Scene series may add a location and year when the user requests them or provides them.
- Use a restrained editorial title, usually 1–4 words, with a light weight and generous tracking.
- Never invent a date, location, year, brand, logo, URL, or call to action.
- Preserve user-supplied titles, names, places, and years as written; do not translate them unless asked.
- If the user requests no text, remove the entire information layer.
- Keep all text in the lower-half negative space and away from the key subject.

## Final quality check

Before returning the result, verify that:

- the top half still reads as the real photograph;
- the split and overall ratio are close to 50/50 and 3:4;
- the lower watercolor is medium-sized, airy, and not a full-scene copy;
- people, relationships, center of gravity, limb directions, and motion remain recognizable;
- motion blur follows the real action direction;
- paper, transparency, unfinished edges, and negative space remain visible;
- text is correct, minimal, and free of invented facts;
- there are no heavy frames, dense backgrounds, cartoon aesthetics, 3D rendering, or excessive splashes.
