# Watercolor Memory Poster — Art Direction

## Watercolor movement

Use delicate traditional watercolor. Keep it light, translucent, airy, imperfect, and restrained. Do not fully paint every surface. Let paper white remain visible inside and around the subjects.

When movement is present, preserve and gently amplify it with controlled watercolor motion blur. Use directional brush dragging, broken pigment edges, translucent overlapping washes, partially dissolved contours, dry-brush streaks, soft trailing pigment, subtle repeated gesture traces, disappearing edges, flowing hair strokes, fabric movement, and softly blurred moving limbs.

Motion blur must follow the real physical direction of the photographed action. Keep the face, body center, essential gesture, and important object relatively recognizable. Allow secondary moving areas to dissolve.

Examples:
- running: trail hair, jacket edges, arms, and legs backward;
- turning: sweep hair and clothing around the body;
- jumping: let legs and fabric dissolve vertically;
- swinging: create a directional watercolor arc through arms and object;
- walking: let feet and lower clothing fade into loose brush movement.

The effect must feel like **movement itself passing through watercolor**, not a static watercolor portrait of a moving person.

For calm standing or walking scenes, keep motion effects subtle. For running, jumping, waving, swinging, spinning, or other energetic actions, allow more expressive flow.

## Emotion and expression

Do not sacrifice expression for painterly style. Preserve the emotional character of the photograph.

- laughing: retain joyful openness;
- running: retain energy and forward momentum;
- waving: preserve the raised hand and spontaneous gesture;
- looking back: preserve the relationship between gaze and body direction;
- interacting: preserve the emotional connection and relative positioning between people.

The lower illustration should immediately remind the viewer of the exact captured moment.

## Environment

Do not reproduce the full background. Reduce it to a few poetic clues and let them dissolve into the paper.

Examples:
- crosswalk: several pale horizontal strokes;
- football field: one soft green wash;
- road: two or three gray directional strokes;
- railing: one thin broken line;
- buildings: faint geometric silhouettes;
- trees: several soft pigment shapes;
- stadium lights: tiny diffused dots;
- traditional wall: a pale gray-beige architectural wash;
- narrow path: one or two linear strokes leading through the scene.

Never create a rectangular painted backdrop, complete scenery block, hard frame, or artificial vignette behind the watercolor.

## Paper and material

Use warm white or pale ivory natural watercolor paper with subtle visible grain. Keep the paper itself visually important.

Use transparent pigment, soft pooling, dry-brush texture, irregular organic edges, subtle granulation, and occasional unfinished brush marks. Avoid excessive splashes or decorative effects unrelated to movement or atmosphere.

## Color

Extract colors from the original photograph and compress them into roughly 3-5 dominant colors. Keep saturation restrained. The watercolor should feel lighter and quieter than the photograph.

Preserve recognizable clothing colors when important, but soften them:
- blue denim -> muted denim blue;
- red jersey -> restrained warm red;
- green field -> pale sage or grass green;
- black clothing -> charcoal gray rather than solid black;
- warm sunset -> diluted ochre, amber, and pale peach.

Let colors fade naturally into the paper. Never turn the lower half into a dense, fully colored painting.

## Composition

Do not mechanically center every person. Analyze the direction of movement and leave more negative space in front of or around the motion when appropriate.

For multiple people, preserve original relative scale, distance, direction, and emotional relationship. They may be brought slightly closer only if necessary for the composition, but the original narrative relationship must remain recognizable.

The composition should feel effortless, quiet, and poetic.

## Typography

Use exactly one line of text. No year. No subtitle. No description. No second line. No extra location label unless the location itself is the single title.

Choose a short English phrase, usually 1-4 words, based on the image's mood and action. Examples:

- IN THE MOMENT
- ON THE MOVE
- IN MOTION
- PASSING BY
- RUNNING FREE
- BETWEEN MOMENTS
- IN THE LIGHT
- AFTER THE RAIN
- JUST LIKE THAT
- TOGETHER
- ON THE GREEN
- TAKE FLIGHT
- SUMMER AIR
- HERE & NOW

Do not automatically reuse the same phrase. Set the text small, quiet, widely spaced, refined, editorial, and understated. Place it naturally in lower negative space and never let it compete with the illustration.

## Visual language

Quiet. Poetic. Airy. Human. Spontaneous. Refined. Minimal. Emotional. Editorial. Handmade. Imperfect. Light. Dynamic. Nostalgic. Premium.

The strongest contrast should be:
- TOP: a real, vivid, spontaneous moment;
- BOTTOM: the same moment becoming lighter, quieter, softer, and more fleeting.

## Avoid

Do not use:
- heavy watercolor coverage;
- dark saturated watercolor;
- dense backgrounds;
- full-scene watercolor reproduction;
- large characters filling the lower half;
- commercial illustration;
- anime or cute cartoon aesthetics;
- smooth digital-painting or vector aesthetics;
- colored pencil or crayon;
- heavy oil painting;
- 3D rendering;
- excessive watercolor splashes;
- random motion blur;
- generic comic speed lines;
- overly detailed faces in the watercolor;
- rigid traced outlines;
- busy compositions;
- decorative frames;
- multiple text blocks;
- years, subtitles, captions, or logos added to the lower design.

## Final art direction

The top half should communicate: **This is what happened.**

The bottom half should communicate: **This is how the moment felt.**

Do not simply redraw the photograph. Extract its emotional center. Preserve the people essential to that moment, their gesture, expression, interaction, and direction of movement. Let everything unnecessary gradually disappear into watercolor, motion, paper, and empty space.
